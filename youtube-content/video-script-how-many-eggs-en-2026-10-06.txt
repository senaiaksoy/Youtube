Being a fertility doctor at a dinner party goes like this. Someone leans in, looks left, looks right, and whispers: "Quick question… how many eggs should I freeze?"

And I have to give the most annoying answer in medicine: "It depends."

Mostly on two things: your age when you freeze, and how many eggs you freeze.

A big 2024 review found this: of women who froze at 35 or younger and later used their eggs, about 5 out of 10 had a baby. Froze at 40 or older? About 2 out of 10.

If that second number is hard to hear: these are averages, not your forecast.

In this video: the numbers at each age, why twice the eggs isn't twice the chance, how IVF changes things, and three questions to ask your clinic. I'm Dr. Senai Aksoy, fertility specialist. Okay — let's go.

First, a confession on behalf of my whole profession: doctors say "it depends" so often it should be printed on our diplomas. So this time, let me actually earn it.

Three words we need to get right: eggs, embryos and babies. They're not the same thing.

When I say "eggs" in this video, I mean mature eggs — the ones that are ready to be fertilized. Not every egg that's collected is mature.

Here's a good way to think about freezing: it's like taking a photo, not buying a time machine. The eggs stay the age they were on the day you froze them. Studies so far show they don't get older in the freezer — but they don't get any younger, either.

Quick question. If you freeze 20 eggs, do you have 20 chances at a baby?

Not quite. Think of it like a talent show. Twenty eggs walk on stage.

Round one: the thaw. Most of them make it.

Round two: fertilization. A few more go home.

Round three: growing into a day-five embryo. Here, a lot more go home.

And then the final round. The chromosome round. A tough judge — and the older the eggs, the tougher it gets.

Here's the number to remember. At one large egg-freezing clinic, if you froze before 38, about 2 or 3 out of every 10 thawed eggs made it all the way to a chromosomally normal embryo. If you froze at 38 or later, it was less than 1 out of 10.

And even the winners don't always get the record deal. A chromosomally normal embryo doesn't always implant.

Bottom line: eggs, embryos and babies are three different numbers — and age shapes all three.

Now, the question you came for. How many eggs do you need at your age?

Every family has one. The uncle with a number. "Eggs? Freeze twenty. Done. My colleague's daughter did it."

With respect: your uncle has never seen an egg that wasn't on a breakfast plate.

One story isn't a statistic. So here's an actual statistic. A university clinic in New York looked at women who froze their eggs and later came back to use them, and counted how many had a baby or an ongoing pregnancy — by their age at freezing, and by how many mature eggs were thawed.

Before I tell you: if you froze before 38 and later thawed 20 or more eggs, how many out of 10 women do you think had a baby? Take a guess.

Most of them did. With 20 or more mature eggs, about 7 or 8 out of 10 women under 38. At 38 to 40, about 5 out of 10. At 41 or older, about 3 or 4 out of 10.

With fewer than 10 eggs, every one of those numbers was much lower. The full table is on screen.

One honest caveat: this is one clinic, some of these groups were small, and other clinics will have different numbers. So treat this as a rough guide, not a promise.

Now, more eggs help — but doubling your eggs doesn't double your chance. In that same clinic's model, at 34, going from 10 to 20 mature eggs took the chance from about 5 out of 10 to nearly 7.

Pause for a second. What matters more: how many cycles you do, or how many mature eggs you end up with in total?

The total. In that study, extra cycles didn't help on their own. They helped because they added eggs.

So should you put all your eggs in one basket? …Yes, that was a dad joke. Occupational hazard. But it's genuinely the point: what counts is the total number of mature eggs, not how many baskets it took.

In short: age sets the odds for each egg, and the total number of eggs decides how many tries you get at those odds.

Now, IVF. Here the eggs aren't frozen and waiting; they're fertilized right away. Same logic, different timing.

In a huge UK analysis, the predicted chance of a baby from a fresh IVF cycle rose with the number of eggs collected — mature or not — up to about 15. Between 15 and 20 it flattened out, and beyond 20 it dipped. I'll spare you the spreadsheet — the details are on screen.

But that's only the fresh transfer. Count the frozen embryos from the same collection too, and in another large study the chance kept climbing with more eggs — more slowly at the top, but with no flattening.

Now, the serious part. Stimulation isn't risk-free. Pushing for a very high number of eggs raises the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation. The goal is a sensible number for you — not a personal record.

So: in IVF too, more eggs help — up to a number that's safe for you.

A few things worth knowing before you decide.

Freezing eggs isn't a guarantee of a baby. Remember: it's a photo, not a time machine. It can improve your odds later — mostly when the eggs are frozen younger.

Babies born from frozen eggs appear to do as well at birth as babies born from fresh eggs.

In the studies we looked at, only about 1 out of 10 women came back to use their eggs within the first several years.

And the process itself — hormone injections and an egg retrieval — takes time and money, and carries its own risks. That deserves a proper conversation with your doctor.

If you ask me what I take from all this — and this is my clinical view, not a study result — three things matter more than any single round number. Your age when the eggs are frozen. The total number of mature eggs you end up with. And your own lab's results. If you're thinking about freezing, earlier tends to be kinder to the odds. And whatever your age, you deserve honest numbers, not a promise.

So, back to the dinner party. "How many eggs should I freeze?" It still depends. But now you know on what: mostly your age when you freeze, and how many mature eggs you have in total. And twice the eggs isn't twice the chance.

If you remember one thing, remember the photo. And next time your uncle says "Freeze twenty. Done." Just ask him: how old is the photo?

When you see your doctor, here are three questions you can bring. How many mature eggs would you expect me to get per cycle, at my age and with my test results? What's your lab's survival rate after thawing, and how many women my age have had a baby from their frozen eggs here? And if one cycle gives fewer eggs than we hoped, what would a second cycle realistically add? I've put these in the description too.
