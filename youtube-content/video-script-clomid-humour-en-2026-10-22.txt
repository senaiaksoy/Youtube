You're taking Clomid, but still no pregnancy. And suddenly it's a full-blown household crisis: "Do we start a new box, or do we change doctors?"

Before you sprint to the late-night pharmacy, there's one question to answer first: did this treatment actually trigger ovulation, yes or no? Because not ovulating at all, and ovulating perfectly without getting pregnant, are two completely different stories. It's the difference between "the car won't even start" and "the engine runs beautifully, but somebody forgot to release the parking brake."

I'm Dr. Senai Aksoy. Today we'll look at why this medication has its limits, and when you need to be able to say: "Okay, folks, this tactic isn't working. We're changing the plan."

Picture the kitchen table: the salt shaker, the box of pills, and that famous magnetic calendar on the fridge. The days are ticked off in neon highlighter; it looks like NASA's countdown for a launch to Mars. Romance? Zero. It's a military operation. And the little inner voice kicks in: "It didn't work last month. It's fate... Fine, I'll start another box."

So, we congratulate the calendar on its discipline. But it has one small flaw: it can't read a hormone panel! If we listen to it, we'll carry on like this until the 2028 Olympics.

The active ingredient, clomiphene citrate, actually plays a little trick on the brain. It scrambles the estrogen sensors, and the brain thinks: "Oh no, there's no estrogen down there. Has the shop closed or what?!" So it raises FSH. That's the hormone that makes the follicle grow, that little pocket that holds the egg, all the way to ovulation.

But let's bust a myth right away: a bigger follicle doesn't mean a better egg. Put a completely blurry passport photo in a huge gilded frame, like a painting in the Louvre, and the photo doesn't suddenly turn 4K. Nothing shows that clomiphene improves egg quality. It just bangs on the door, shouting: "Come on, somebody has to come out!"

First scenario: you took the pills, but zero ovulation.

Instead of opening another box, you need to find out who's on strike in there. Polycystic ovary syndrome, PCOS? The thyroid or prolactin, playing a solo outside the orchestra? Or maybe, on the "get bikini-ready" diet, one lettuce leaf a day and three hours of intense exercise, the brain said: "Famine alert! Cut the breaker on the reproductive system, we're saving our own skin!"?

Each cause has its own treatment. If the missing ovulation is linked to PCOS, with no other fertility factor, the 2023 international guidelines place letrozole first-line. That doesn't mean Clomid is retired: the choice depends on the diagnosis, your previous response, and your country's prescribing rules. And please, no self-medicating on advice from the tea-and-gossip crowd: "My sister-in-law took it and had triplets, so take two pills, sweetheart!"

Second scenario: ovulation clearly happened, but still no pregnancy.

Here, the medication tells you: "I got the egg to the exit door. What happens next isn't my department." And it's right! Ovulation is just the movie trailer.

Clomid is not a plumber, it won't unblock a blocked tube! And when she takes the treatment, his sperm, lounging on the couch watching the game highlights, don't suddenly turn into Michael Phelps. No, medicine hasn't received that Bluetooth update yet! The workup involves the couple, and age and good timing matter just as much.

And by the way, take women who already ovulate like Swiss clockwork but have unexplained infertility. Giving them clomiphene alone, with a cheerful "Set an alarm on your phone, tonight we're working overtime," hasn't been shown to improve the chances of pregnancy. When the factory is already running, there's no point shaking the manager every five minutes. Stimulating the ovaries for an insemination, though? That's a different story.

So, how many months do we keep trying?

The French prescribing information sets a maximum of six treatment cycles. But careful: that number six isn't a loyalty card, "buy five coffees, get the sixth free," that you absolutely have to get stamped to the end. If three courses at the recommended dose haven't produced ovulation, the trial is considered over. And if ovulation happens but there's still no pregnancy after several tries, we review the workup and the strategy, sometimes sooner. Injections, insemination or IVF aren't levels in a video game that you have to clear one by one, in order. We take the route that fits your couple.

And a sentence I hear a lot: "I had three pills left at the bottom of last month's box. It seemed a shame to waste them, so I took them."
Come on, it's not half a lemon forgotten in the fridge door! Having pills lying around is not a medical indication.

Proper monitoring means ultrasound, to watch the follicles and the lining of the uterus, and hormone tests can help assess ovulation. Because clomiphene has a peculiar sense of humor. By blocking estrogen receptors, it can thin the uterine lining, the endometrium, right where the baby needs to settle in. It's like insisting to your guests, "No, really, stay the night!" and while they're taking off their shoes in the hallway, you sell all the beds. Or the other way around: several follicles take off at the same time, and hello, risk of multiple pregnancy.

Now, two minutes without jokes, for safety.

Hot flashes, headaches or mild abdominal discomfort are possible side effects. But blurred vision, flashes of light, spots in front of your eyes, or any other unusual visual disturbance... never tell yourself, "It's just low blood pressure." Stop the treatment and contact your doctor promptly. A full eye exam is needed. If nothing else explains these symptoms, clomiphene must be stopped permanently. Don't restart it on your own.

Same goes for severe abdominal pain, rapid swelling of the belly, or shortness of breath: that's a medical emergency. Don't wait for your next appointment. And of course, before every new treatment cycle, we check that a pregnancy hasn't already started.

If it didn't work with Clomid, it's not your fault. At your next appointment, simply ask your doctor these three questions:

"Have I really ovulated?"

"What other factors have we checked?"

"At what point do we move to plan B?"

It's not the calendar on the fridge that should decide whether to open a new box, but the answers to those three questions.

This information doesn't replace a medical consultation. The medication, the dose and the length of treatment are decided with the doctor who follows you.
