Question one: the mystery of the weeks. A blastocyst — a five-day embryo — was transferred exactly sixteen days ago. You count on your fingers: sixteen days is barely more than two weeks. And then the doctor calmly announces:

"Congratulations, you're five weeks pregnant!"

Excuse me? You count again, with your toes. Who added three weeks? Has math changed since school?

No, nobody cheated, and math hasn't changed. Welcome to obstetrics: the only field in the world where your contract starts two weeks before you've even met the employer! Two weeks of seniority, straight away, on the house.

We count the weeks of pregnancy from the first day of your last period — that's about two weeks before fertilization. Even when it all started in a dish in the lab, under the watchful eye of the embryologist, we keep that calendar. It's the Greenwich Mean Time of pregnancy: not necessarily your time zone, but every doctor sets their watch by it.

With IVF, we know the exact date to the day, but we still convert it. On the day a five-day blastocyst is transferred, you're already two weeks and five days pregnant. With a three-day embryo, two weeks and three days. Two calendar weeks, plus your blastocyst's five days: that's where your mysterious almost-three weeks come from.

And in France, to make things even trickier, there's also a second count, called "S-G" in French, which starts at fertilization. So, two weeks less. Two different clocks for a single uterus. To spare you the headache, I'm sticking to the count from your last period.

Key point: always ask your doctor how many weeks pregnant you are, counted from your last period, and keep the transfer date and the embryo's age somewhere safe. Every ultrasound milestone is counted that way.

Question two: what should we see, and when?

At this stage, the scan is usually done vaginally. The probe is right next to the uterus, so it sees things much earlier than through the belly.

On screen, at first, it's a bit like looking at clouds: everyone sees what they hope to see. The doctor, though, looks for three very specific landmarks, always in the same order.

One: the gestational sac. A small, round, dark pocket in the uterus. At around five weeks, sometimes just a little earlier.

Two: the yolk sac. Inside, a little ring. Sounds like breakfast. It isn't. Usually a bit after five weeks. When we see the sac with its yolk sac inside the uterus, it's official: we've confirmed at least one pregnancy in the uterus. But after IVF, even though it's extremely rare, we also check there isn't a second one in the wrong place.

Three: the embryo. A few millimeters, and inside it a little flicker: the cardiac activity, usually at around six weeks.

Question three: what if we can't see cardiac activity yet?

Sometimes it's simply too early. Sometimes the pregnancy has stopped developing. And a single early scan can't always tell the difference.

That's why the medical criteria are very strict. Measurements to the millimeter: the size of the sac and of the embryo. Not just impressions.

The UK's NICE guidelines are cautious. When a very early scan can't give an answer, a follow-up scan is often planned. At least seven days later. The decision depends mainly on the size of the sac and of the embryo. And waiting for that scan has no negative effect on how the pregnancy develops. Of course, if you have worrying pain or bleeding, you don't just sit and wait a week.

A large American study, published online in October 2025, adds something new. It looked at IVF pregnancies with a sac clearly visible in the uterus. From six weeks and three days, if there was no embryo, or an embryo without cardiac activity, the pregnancy did not continue, in every case they observed. That's an important result. But it's a retrospective study, from a single center, and these cut-offs aren't universal criteria yet.

If your team suggests a follow-up scan, it's not to drag out the suspense. It's to avoid a wrong diagnosis and get a reliable answer. I know full well that this week of waiting feels like an eternity.

Key point: right at the start, an incomplete picture isn't enough to conclude. The follow-up scan is there to clear up the doubt.

And if the cardiac activity is visible? That's excellent news, a real milestone. But let's talk numbers, honestly. What's your guess: out of ten IVF pregnancies where we've seen the heartbeat, how many keep going?

These are broad statistical benchmarks, not your individual situation. An older American study, from a single center, followed more than two thousand IVF pregnancies with cardiac activity seen at around seven weeks. Under 35, more than 9 in 10 kept developing. Between 35 and 39, a little under 9 in 10. And from 40 on, a little under 8 in 10. A Chinese study published in 2016 found a similar picture. In more than two thousand five hundred IVF pregnancies where the heartbeat had been seen, about 9 in 10 got past the first trimester. And there too, the risk went up with age.

Some of these numbers are a reminder to stay cautious. But they describe groups, not your personal story. Seeing that heartbeat is a major milestone. Not a promise carved in stone, but a huge step forward.

Now, safety. Straight talk.

A pregnancy can sometimes implant outside the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube: that's an ectopic pregnancy. In large IVF statistics, it affects about 1 to 2 pregnancies in 100, and even fewer after transferring a single frozen blastocyst. The risk is higher if your tubes have been damaged or operated on, or if you've had an ectopic pregnancy before. The early scan is there precisely to check where the pregnancy implanted.

And much more rarely, there can be two pregnancies at the same time: one in the uterus, another outside it. That's called a heterotopic pregnancy. With IVF, it's a few cases per thousand, or fewer. That's why the doctor also checks both sides of the uterus.

So, even after a reassuring scan that morning, some signs should never wait for the next appointment. Sharp pain in the lower belly, especially on one side. Unusual pain at the tip of the shoulder. Feeling faint, or passing out. Or heavy bleeding.

In those cases, you don't ask the internet. You go straight to the emergency department, or call 15 in France, one-one-two anywhere in Europe, or your local emergency number.

For light bleeding or brown discharge, contact your center. For persistent pain, even moderate, call your team the same day as well.

And your support medications, progesterone included: keep taking them exactly as prescribed, until the exact date your center gave you. Even after a beautiful image, you never stop a treatment on your own.
